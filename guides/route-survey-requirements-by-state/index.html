Which states require a route survey?

A route survey is a physical drive of the route, measuring every bridge, wire and signal the load will pass under. Here is the height and width at which each state requires one.

Updated September 2026 · Figures from FreightDeck's audit of 51 US jurisdictions and its monthly market-rate check

The table

50 of 51 states publish a route survey trigger. The most common height trigger is 16 ft (13 states) and the most common width trigger is 16 ft (16 states). The strictest: New Hampshire at 13 ft 6 in high, Massachusetts at 13 ft 8 in high, Connecticut at 14 ft high.

District of Columbia publish no fixed trigger and require surveys case by case with the permit. Several states also require a survey for any superload regardless of dimension. A load over the trigger in any one state on the route needs a survey for that state's miles.

What the survey changes

  • Lead time. The survey has to be done before the permit is issued, and it takes a day or two of driving plus a report. It is the long pole on a superload schedule. Permit lead times by state →
  • Route. The surveyor often finds the obstruction that forces a detour, an escort change or a utility lift. That is the point: better on paper than under a wire.
  • Cost. Surveys are priced flat or per mile depending on the state and the surveyor. What a route survey costs →

Questions

Which states require a route survey?

50 states, once a load exceeds a height (most often 16 ft) or width (most often 16 ft) trigger. See the table for each state.

Who does a route survey?

Usually the pilot car company or a dedicated survey firm, driving the exact permitted route with a height pole and recording every clearance.
