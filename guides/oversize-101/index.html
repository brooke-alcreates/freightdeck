FreightDeck · Oversize Freight 101

Oversize freight, explained properly.

Anything wider than 8'6", taller than a state's height limit, longer than its combination limit, or heavier than 80,000 lbs gross is over-dimensional. It needs a permit in every state it crosses, often an escort, and a plan. This guide covers how that world actually works, with the real numbers. Written for the desk quoting its first oversize load, and useful long after.

10 chapters Figures audited across 62 jurisdictions
01

The rules are state rules

The single most important thing to understand about oversize freight: there is no national oversize permit. The federal government sets a floor; each state owns everything above it.

What Washington sets

  • 102 inches (8'6") wide on the National Network of highways, from the Surface Transportation Assistance Act of 1982. States cannot go stricter on those roads.
  • 80,000 lbs gross, 20,000 lbs on a single axle, 34,000 lbs on a tandem, on the Interstate.
  • Bridge Formula B, which caps weight based on how far apart your axles are: W = 500(LN⁄(N−1) + 12N + 36). More axles, more spread, more legal weight.
  • No federal height limit at all. Height is purely a state matter.

What the states own

  • Height limits: 13'6" or 14' depending on the state.
  • Every oversize and overweight permit: fees, forms, processing times.
  • Escort triggers: at what width, height, or length a pilot car is required.
  • Travel restrictions: daylight rules, weekend bans, holiday blackouts.
  • Superload thresholds and the engineering review behind them.
Field note Permits are per state, not per load. A move from Mobile, AL to Seattle, WA crosses six or more states. Each one issues its own permit, charges its own fee, sets its own escort rules, and can hold its own timeline. The strictest state on the route sets your real constraints.
Why learn this now The freight that breaks the rules is the freight that's growing. Wind blades now run 200 to 300 feet, and global wind installations jumped roughly 40% in 2025 alone. Power-transformer lead times have stretched from about 30 weeks in 2019 to well over two years amid the grid and data-center buildout, and when a transformer finally ships, it ships as heavy haul. The desks that can price this freight accurately are the ones that win it.

If a 13'6" state sits on your route, sometimes the permit costs more than the detour fuel.

Watch the axles A load can be under 80,000 lbs and still be overweight. Axle limits bite before gross does. 60,000 lbs sitting over one tandem group is illegal even though the gross is fine, and that's what the scale house checks. Weight distribution across axles, not the total, is what determines whether a heavy load is legal, and it's why heavy hauls add axles instead of just bigger trailers.

Trailer math

Road height is deck height plus cargo height. The trailer choice decides whether a tall load needs a permit chain or moves as ordinary legal freight.

Standard flatbed

60" deck

Legal cargo up to 8'6" tall in 13'6" states. Pipe, steel, lumber, machinery that loads by crane.

Step deck

36" deck

Legal cargo up to 10'6" tall. Tall machinery without the lowboy day rate.

RGN / lowboy

18–24" well

Legal cargo up to 11'6"–12' tall. The gooseneck detaches so tracked equipment drives on.

04

Permits: what they cost, how they work

The permit itself is usually the cheapest line on the quote. What matters is the process around it: who files, what the permit actually says, and the clock that starts when it issues.

Oversize (OS)

  • Any dimension over the state's legal limit
  • Base single-trip fee: a flat fee on each state’s published schedule, usually the smallest line on the quote
  • Routine issue: same day to 2 business days in most states
  • The load must be non-divisible: if it can reasonably be broken down to legal size, states expect you to break it down

Overweight (OW)

  • Gross over 80,000 lbs, or any axle group over its limit
  • Weight-and-distance fees stack on the base: on a genuinely heavy load the weight charge usually outweighs the base fee
  • Texas example: a base fee plus a weight-bracket charge that climbs with gross weight
  • Axle configuration is part of the application. The state is checking bridges, not just the total

What a first-timer doesn't know about the permit itself

  • The carrier pulls the permit in nearly every case. Permits are issued to the specific vehicle and carrier running the load, so the carrier applies. A broker will occasionally file on a carrier's behalf, but it's rare, and even then the permit names the carrier and truck. As the broker or shipper, your job is to price the permits and verify they exist before the wheels turn.
  • The permit specifies the route. Not "I-40 west," the actual roads. Deviate from the permitted route and the permit is void, along with the insurance story if anything happens.
  • Most desks use a permit service. Billed per state, more for complex permits. They file, chase approvals, and know each state's quirks. Filing direct with every DOT works, but it's a job.
  • Wrong dimensions void the permit. If the load is bigger than the paper says, the fine is the least of the problem: the move is uninsured against the permit.
Curveball Permit clocks are tighter than you think. A single-trip permit is valid for 5 days in 33 states, and Connecticut and Louisiana give you just 3. Build a six-day transit starting Wednesday and the clock kills the permit before you clear the state. Check validity windows against your travel-day calendar, not the wall calendar. The lenient end: Texas, Florida, and Georgia run 10 days, Hawaii 30.
Exemptions exist Some commodities skip permits entirely in certain states. Farm equipment, manufactured homes, and forest products are the big three. Iowa exempts implements of husbandry at any width off the Interstate; Illinois caps its farm exemption at 16' wide, daylight only, state and local roads. Same baler, different rulebook. Check the commodity rule before quoting; FreightDeck flags these per state automatically.
05

Escorts: pilot cars, pole cars, police

Past a certain size the load can't move alone. Three kinds of escort, three different jobs, three different prices. The triggers are state lines, and they vary more than any other rule in this guide.

Pilot car width & length

A civilian escort vehicle ahead or behind the load. Warns oncoming traffic, calls out obstructions, blocks lanes through pinch points.

Billingper mile, day minimum

One car typically from 12' wide; two cars from around 14'. Convention: the escort runs in front on two-lane roads, behind on divided highway.

Pole car height

A front escort carrying a flexible height pole set a few inches above load height. If the pole hits a wire or a bridge, the load stops before the cargo does.

Billingper mile, higher than a pilot car

Required for tall loads; the trigger height is state-set and spans more than four feet.

Police escort the wildcard

State troopers or county deputies, mandated by the DOT above certain sizes, most commonly 16'–20' wide.

Billingofficer's hourly rate, minimum call-out

The price is the officer's rate, often overtime, not a DOT fee schedule. Some states publish it; most do not.

Curveball The same 14'2" load needs a pole car in Vermont, but not in Texas. Massachusetts has the strictest high-pole trigger in the country at 13'8". New York and Vermont trigger at 14'. A big cluster (CT, FL, IL, IN, IA, MD, MI, OH, OR, PA, WA) sits at 14'6". Texas, Kansas, and Montana don't trigger until 17', North Dakota 18', and five states (CA, MN, NH, RI, SD) have no high-pole trigger at all. A 14'2" load is routine in the South and a pole-car load in New England. Check the state, not the load.
Certification Escorting is a licensed trade in about 14 states, including TX, FL, NY, PA, GA, NC, WA, and CO. Eight of them honor each other's certification through a reciprocity compact. New York honors nobody's: an escort certified in thirteen other states is a rookie in New York until they pass the state's own test. Certified escorts in a hot corridor are a real scheduling constraint, so book them when you book the truck. The gear is standardized too: the yellow OVERSIZE LOAD banner, corner flags, and amber warning lights are spelled out state by state.

06

Route surveys

Above certain dimensions, someone has to physically drive the route before the load does: measuring bridges, wires, and turns with a tape measure and a notebook. States set the trigger; the spread is enormous.

What gets checked

  • Bridge and underpass clearances, vertical and side
  • Utility lines, traffic signals, sign gantries
  • Turning radii at intersections and ramps
  • Construction zones and active detours
  • Railroad crossings, including wire heights

The facts

  • Who does it: an escort or pilot-car company, not the DOT
  • Cost: priced flat or per mile by the surveyor; FreightDeck estimates it per route
  • DOT engineering review adds a state fee where required
  • Timing: it happens before the permit can issue, so it's on the critical path

Curveball A 13'9" load needs a route survey in New Hampshire and Massachusetts, and nowhere else in the country. Desks that don't know the spread either quote surveys everywhere and lose the bid, or quote them nowhere and lose the margin. The survey bill follows the strict states on the route, not the load.
Why surveys exist A height permit does not guarantee clearance. The state permits the dimension; it does not certify that every wire and bridge on the route clears it. The electrical safety code only requires about 18 feet of clearance for many lines over public roads, and older or sagging lines run lower. That gap between "permitted" and "clears" is exactly what the route survey and the high-pole car are for.

Curveball What counts as a superload depends on the state, not the truck. Maine and Montana call it a superload at 100,000 lbs. Indiana at 108k, Ohio at 120k, Illinois at 150k, Florida at 199k, Texas at 254,300. Alabama and Utah hold out to 300,000 lbs. A 200k-lb transformer is a heavy but routine overweight move through Texas and a full engineering project through Ohio. Same truck, same load, different worlds.

08

When the load can move

An oversize permit isn't permission to drive whenever. Most states restrict OD travel to daylight, many restrict weekends, and every one of them blacks out major holidays. Transit time is a calendar problem, not a mileage problem.

Daylight rule
35 states
Daylight-only for OD: typically half an hour before sunrise to half an hour after sunset
December daylight ≈ 9 road hours
Realistic pace
~350 mi/day
Daylight-limited, escort-paced. A 1,500-mile route is a 5-travel-day move
Legal freight does 550+

8 states
AL, FL, IN, LA, MD, MI, PA, RI restrict weekend OD travel

Holiday blackouts

Virtually every state bans OD travel on the big six: New Year's, Memorial Day, July 4th, Labor Day, Thanksgiving, Christmas. Most extend the blackout from noon the day before to sunrise the day after; some northeastern states add Good Friday or Easter. A holiday inside your transit window can cost two to three calendar days.

Urban curfews stack on top. Many metros bar OD moves through rush hours (California cities run roughly 6–9 AM and 3–6 PM), and Texas restricts superloads in the Houston and Dallas corridors overnight. Weather counts too: most states halt OD movement when visibility drops below about 1,000 feet. The permit lists the windows; read them.

Field note Violating a travel window voids the permit. That means state fines, possible impoundment, and a liability story nobody wants if the carrier was moving outside the permitted hours. Confirm restrictions per state on the route, not just at the origin.

The checklist before you quote

  • Get exact dimensions from the shipper, not rounded. 8'6" and 8'7" are different worlds.
  • Ask for loaded height: cargo plus the deck it rides on, and overall length including overhang.
  • Confirm the load is non-divisible.
  • Count travel days against daylight windows, weekends, and holidays, not just miles.
  • Anything over 14' tall or 100,000 lbs: verify the permit path before you commit a number.

The classic first-timer mistakes

  • Measuring the cargo, not the ride. A 9' crate is legal in a warehouse and 14' tall on a flatbed. Height is measured ground to top of the loaded trailer.
  • Trusting the spec sheet's "about." Dimensions on a purchase order are routinely wrong. The cheapest insurance in heavy haul is a tape measure on the loaded truck before the driver leaves origin.
  • Quoting miles, not days. Daylight-only pace is the schedule, and detention math follows it.
  • Assuming the weekend. Eight states restrict it; holiday weekends stall a load for days.
  • Skipping the escort call. Escort availability is a real constraint in rural corridors; book early.

Talk the language

The vocabulary that gets used on the phone.
